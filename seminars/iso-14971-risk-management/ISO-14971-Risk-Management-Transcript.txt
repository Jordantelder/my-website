Nobody Fails an Audit for Not Owning the Standard

There is an asymmetry at the heart of most risk-management findings, and I want to name it before we go anywhere else. Manufacturers prepare one set of deliverables, reviewers do a different set of things entirely, and the space between the two is where findings come from.

What manufacturers prepare typically includes: a procedure that restates the standard clause by clause, a spreadsheet with a color matrix, a risk management report signed at design transfer, and a plan that was written after the analysis was already finished. Each of those is a real deliverable and each one is auditable. However, not one of them is what a reviewer does when the file is in front of them.

A reviewer pulls one hazard and walks it end to end, asks where a probability number came from, asks for the effectiveness record rather than the implementation record, and asks what post-market data changed after the file was signed. Those moves are broadly the same in a notified body audit and in an FDA inspection.

Traceability breaks at the control, estimates have no stated basis, the Clause 10 log is either empty or entirely historical, and the acceptability criteria turn out to have been reverse-engineered from the scores the team already had. Risk files very rarely fail because a procedure is missing. They fail on traceability, and on arithmetic that cannot be defended.

The File Is the Argument

Understanding what ISO 14971:2019 actually requires of a risk file is where this session has to start, and we have 13 minutes here. The aim is to fix the 4 places where risk files reliably break in practice, which are the working versions of the gap I showed you a moment ago, and they include: the plan written too late, the chain collapsed into a single row, the estimate recorded with no stated basis, and the file that is a folder nobody can walk. There are 10 clauses in the standard, and they carry one obligation between them, which is to make every risk decision traceable and defensible.


SLIDE 6

A File, Not a Folder

Clause 3.25 is deliberately thin, and it is the right place to start, because it defines the risk management file as the set of records and other documents that are produced by risk management. There is no list of contents, no prescribed format, and no template, which means the content requirements are distributed through the standard, and almost every clause ends with a sentence sending its own output into the file.

Clause 4.5 is where the actual obligation sits. It requires the file to provide traceability for each identified hazard to the risk analysis, the risk evaluation, the risk control measures, and the assessment of the acceptability of any residual risk. That is the sentence that turns a folder into a file, and it is the sentence manufacturers fail.

The file may be a set of references to other records, so manufacturers do not have to physically bind the usability engineering file into it. What the file may not be is unwalkable. The 'RM File Index' sheet in your toolkit is that index, and it is there so that somebody who has never seen your device can move from a single hazard out to every decision you made about it.

The questions a reviewer arrives with are predictable, and the file has to be able to answer each of them:

- Where did this hazard come from? That is answered from 5.3, characteristics related to safety, and 5.4, hazard identification.
- How did this hazard reach a harm? That is 5.4 again, the foreseeable sequences of events and the resulting hazardous situation.
- Where did this number come from? That is 5.5 risk estimation, together with whatever categorization system you used.
- Why is this acceptable? That draws on your 4.4 d) criteria, Clause 6 risk evaluation, and the 7.3 residual risk evaluation.
- What did you do about it, and does it work? That is 7.1 option analysis, and both of the verifications in 7.2.
- What has changed since? That is 10.2 collection, 10.3 review, and 10.4 actions.

Ten Clauses, and the Three People Skip

The spine of the standard is short enough to walk in under a minute. Clause 4 sets up the system, 5 analyses, 6 evaluates, 7 controls, 8 evaluates the whole, 9 reviews and reports, and 10 keeps the file alive once the device is on the market. You will note in the last column that the usual state is fairly consistent. The procedure exists while the plan underneath it is thin, and the hazard chain in Clause 5 has collapsed with the basis for the estimates gone missing. Clause 6 is done, however the criteria were written late, and Clause 7's order of priority was not worked as an order. Clause 8 has been confused with the sum of the rows, and the Clause 9 report is signed, sometimes early.

I want to stop on the 3 rows in gold, because these are the 3 that are most often simply absent. Clause 7.5 requires manufacturers to review every risk control measure for new hazards and for effects on the existing estimates. Clause 7.6 requires manufacturers to show that every identified hazardous situation has been considered. Additionally, Clause 10 as a whole, production and post-production information, is very often an empty log.

Clause 2 is new in the 2019 edition, and it states that there are no normative references. That is a trap for anyone who assumes that conforming to ISO 14971 pulls in ISO/TR 24971, because it does not. The TR is a technical report, and it is guidance only.


SLIDE 8

Three Definitions That Arrived in 2019

Clause 3 carries 31 definitions, and 3 of them are new in the third edition. None of the 3 is cosmetic, because each one creates an obligation somewhere else in the standard, and each one costs you work.

3.2 defines 'benefit' as a positive impact or desirable outcome of use on the health of an individual, or a positive impact on patient management or on public health. Having a definition at all is what makes Clause 7.4 and Clause 8 workable, because you cannot weigh a benefit you have never characterized. What that costs you is a benefit statement with a nature, a magnitude, a probability, and a duration attached to it, and "it treats the disease" is not a benefit statement.

3.15 defines 'reasonably foreseeable misuse' as use in a way not intended by the manufacturer, but which can result from readily predictable human behaviour. That definition is why 5.2 changed its title to "intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse", and why the use-related analysis now has to reach beyond what you wrote in the instructions for use. Misuse can be intentional, and the workarounds your own service team already knows about are foreseeable by definition.

3.28 defines 'state of the art' as the developed stage of technical capability at a given time, based on consolidated findings of science, technology, and experience. You will note that this is not the most advanced solution available, it is generally accepted good practice. It is an input to your Clause 4.2 policy and a trigger in 10.3, which means a movement in the state of the art ages your file without a single complaint or failure having occurred.

There is some plumbing underneath these definitions that I want to flag. Most of the 2019 definitions are now sourced from ISO/IEC Guide 63:2019 rather than Guide 51, and defined terms are printed in italics in the body text, so you can see when the standard is using a term of art. "Use error" is drawn from IEC 62366-1, which is the textual bridge between this standard and your usability engineering work.

The Plan Decides the Answer Before You Know It

This is the single highest-yield slide in this first section. Clause 4.4 requires 7 things of the risk management plan, a) through g), and every one of them is a decision that has to be made before the analysis is done. The most common finding in this area is a plan whose acceptability criteria were quite clearly written after the risk table had been colored in.

The 7 required elements include:

a) Scope: The plan has to identify and describe the device and the life cycle phases covered, which in practice decides what is in, what is excluded, and for what reason.

b) Responsibilities and Authorities: This is an assignment of authority, and there are 2 authorities in play, because someone signs that an individual residual risk is acceptable and someone signs the overall residual risk.

c) Requirements for Review: These are the milestones that eventually produce your Clause 9 risk management report.

e) A Method to Evaluate Overall Residual Risk: The method and the criteria for its acceptability are both declared here.

f) Verification Activities: These cover verification of both the implementation and the effectiveness of risk control measures.

g) Production and Post-Production Activities: These are the activities for collection and review of relevant production and post-production information.

I want to come back to d), because it is 2 obligations and not one. The first is the matrix, and the policy that sits behind the matrix. Two Regulators, One Standard

So now that we have a baseline understanding of the file and the plan, we can move on to look at what the 2 regulators actually do with this standard, and we will spend the next 12 minutes here. ISO 14971 is the common language of risk management, but it is not the legal requirement in either jurisdiction, because it is not incorporated into US law and it is only narrowly harmonised in the EU. Both regulators still expect it, and the gap between that expectation and the legal text is where findings live.


SLIDE 11

The Legal Position

In order to use any of this in front of an investigator, we have to take the legal position and the practical position separately, because manufacturers draw the wrong conclusion in both directions. ISO 14971 is not incorporated by reference into 21 CFR Part 820. Section 820.7 incorporates ISO 13485:2016, and it does not incorporate ISO 14971. In the Quality Management System Regulation final rule FDA was asked to incorporate ISO 14971 as well and declined, on the basis that the risk management requirements it needs are already captured through ISO 13485. That is the first half of the answer.

The Practical Position

The second half is the half manufacturers miss. Declining to incorporate ISO 14971 did not reduce the obligation, it relocated it. Risk management is enforced through the ISO 13485 clauses that Part 820 does incorporate, and those clauses include:

- Clause 4.1.2 b), which requires a risk-based approach applied to the control of the appropriate quality management system processes. This is an inspectable element in its own right.
- Clause 7.1, which requires risk management in product realization, with documented requirements and records of the results.
- Clauses 7.3 design, 7.4 purchasing, 7.5 production and 7.6 monitoring equipment, each of which carries risk proportionality. Manufacturers are required to size supplier controls, process validation and acceptance activities to the risk.
- Clause 8.2 on feedback, complaints and reporting, which uses post-market information as an input to risk management. This is the FDA analogue of Clause 10.

It is important to note that this list is a general list, and each manufacturer's process architecture will file those records in slightly different places.

Compliance Program 7382.850 lists "Risk-based Approach" under Management Oversight as an inspectable element. So the practical answer to the question of whether ISO 14971 is mandatory for FDA is that the standard is not, and the activity is. An observation is written against the clause, not against ISO 14971, and arguing the first half of that answer in front of an investigator without the second half goes badly.

Recognition and Incorporation

Separately from Part 820, ISO 14971:2019 is an FDA-recognized consensus standard, so conformity to it can be declared in a submission. The entry is recognition number 5-125, ISO 14971 Third edition 2019-12, with the extent of recognition given as the complete standard. It entered the database on 23 December 2019 and was published in Recognition List Number 053 at 85 FR 17584 on 30 March 2020, replacing recognition 5-40 for the 2007 edition. It was still unchanged in Recognition List Number 066 of 24 August 2026. Recognition is not incorporation. This means that the entry supports a submission, however it does not create a regulation. I always recommend checking the entry before you cite it, because recognition numbers and transition notices change.

One more fact makes the point better than any argument of mine. If you search the 78 pages of Compliance Program 7382.850 for the string 14971, you get zero hits. The inspection program is written entirely against ISO 13485 clauses and 21 CFR Part 820, and FDA therefore inspects the activity without ever naming the standard.


SLIDE 12

Harmonised Status Under the MDR

Writing a GSPR matrix that holds up depends on knowing exactly how far the harmonised standard actually reaches, and that is narrower than most manufacturers assume. EN ISO 14971:2019, as amended by A11:2021, is a harmonised standard for the MDR. The citation is Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2022/757 of 11 May 2022, published in the Official Journal L 138 on 17 May 2022. It is also harmonised for the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation, or IVDR, by Decision 2022/729, and both citations were still live on the Commission's summary list generated on 17 June 2026, with no end of legal effect.

Two facts here reliably surprise people. The first is that there was no harmonised risk management standard under the MDR between 26 May 2021 and 17 May 2022, because the base decision did not cite EN ISO 14971 at all, as A11 did not yet exist. Additionally, A11:2021 does not change a word of the normative text, which is to say it replaces the European foreword and adds Annex ZA for the MDR and Annex ZB for the IVDR.

Annex ZA

Now the part that matters for your file. Annex ZA is a correspondence table, and it is narrow, which is what the title of this slide is pointing at. It addresses MDR Annex I Chapter I points 3, 4, 5, 8, and 9, and you will note in the right hand column that points 1, 2, 6 and 7 are not listed as covered, and neither is any requirement in Chapter II or Chapter III. So conformity with the standard gives you a presumption of conformity for the risk management system and the iterative process at point 3, for the risk control measures and the mandatory order of priority at point 4, for use error at point 5, for the minimization requirement at point 8, and for the Annex XVI devices without an intended medical purpose at point 9. It gives you none at all for the intended performance and benefit-risk requirement in point 1, for the definition of "as far as possible" in point 2, for the lifetime of the device at point 6, or for transport and storage at point 7.

There is a common misconception I want to correct before we move on. The 7 content deviations that manufacturers still cite belong to EN ISO 14971:2012, which was withdrawn, with conflicting national standards withdrawn by 30 June 2020. A11:2021 has no content deviations at all, so if your gap analysis is built on the 7 deviations, it is built on a withdrawn standard.

MDCG 2021-5 rev.1, from the Medical Device Coordination Group, or MDCG, is the authority on what an Annex Z is and how far it reaches. An Annex Z identifies the requirements that are not covered, and presumption of conformity can be claimed only for what the Annex Z lists as covered.


SLIDE 13

ALARP and AFAP

There is one distinction in this section that changes how far manufacturers have to go, and it is the difference between ALARP and AFAP. The cleanest way to make it land is by what is absent from the regulation. "ALARP", as low as reasonably practicable, does not appear anywhere in the MDR. Neither does any reference to economic considerations. The old Medical Devices Directive, 93/42/EEC, carried a recital permitting "technical and economical considerations compatible with a high level of protection of health and safety", and that recital was not carried over into the MDR. There is also no "negligible risk" carve-out in Annex I point 8, which requires that all known and foreseeable risks are minimized and acceptable when weighed against the evaluated benefits, and not only the ones sitting above some threshold.

ALARP is the familiar test, and it is familiar for understandable reasons. It comes out of occupational safety regimes and out of the Directive era, it admits cost, effort, and time into the judgment of how far to go, and it produces a defensible stopping point on economic grounds. However, it is not the MDR test. The MDR's only stated limit on how far a risk must be reduced is the one in Annex I point 2, which defines reduction "as far as possible", or AFAP, as reduction without adversely affecting the benefit-risk ratio. It is important to note that cost, schedule and manufacturability are not permitted limits under that provision. In practice the test is bounded by the state of the art, and the hook for that sits in Annex I points 1 and 4 rather than in the definition itself.

I would like to take a moment to discuss the practical consequence, because this is the one that generates findings. A risk your matrix calls acceptable still has to be reduced as far as possible, and "it is in the green zone" is not a stopping argument in the EU. What manufacturers need in the file is a record of the control options considered and why the higher-priority ones were not practicable, on a basis that isn't economic.

So where does the choice between ALARP and AFAP actually live in the standards system? It lives in ISO/TR 24971:2020 Annex C, as guidance on the Clause 4.2 policy for establishing risk acceptability criteria. Annex C sets out the possible elements of that policy and lists the available approaches, which include: ALARP, AFAP, ALARA, and ALAP. The standard does not choose for you, your policy does, and for an EU device the regulation constrains that choice.

It should be noted that there is one asymmetry here. The IVDR has recital 13 tying reduction as far as possible to the generally acknowledged state of the art in medicine, while the MDR has no equivalent recital, and its state of the art hook sits in Annex I points 1 and 4 instead.

Where They Agree, and Where They Differ

Closing this section out, the picture for a manufacturer selling into both markets is calmer than this section may have made it sound, because the overlap is large and the differences are narrow but sharp. On whether ISO 14971 is required, the EU answer is voluntary but harmonised, with a presumption of conformity for the points Annex ZA lists, while for FDA it is not incorporated into Part 820 but is a recognized consensus standard, and therefore declarable in a submission. On where the file is filed, the MDR puts the benefit-risk analysis and the results of risk management inside the technical documentation at Annex II point 5, while FDA expects them in the design and development file, or the medical device file, reviewed at inspection rather than submitted.

On post-market information the routes are different and the evidence is much the same. The EU routes it through Article 83(3)(a), and through the indicators and thresholds at Annex III point 1(b). It also routes it through the PSUR, post-market clinical follow-up, and trend reporting under Article 88. FDA routes it through ISO 13485 Clause 8.2 feedback and complaints, and through Medical Device Reporting under 21 CFR 803, which is a second and entirely different MDR.

There are 3 rows here that carry a genuine difference. Whether benefit-risk is expected for an individual risk is the one that changes your record count. SLIDE 16

The Chain a Reviewer Expects to See

In order to estimate a risk that anybody can argue with, we have to agree on the four positions in the chain, and I am going to draw this on the board as I talk, because the picture is the whole teaching point. A hazard, defined at 3.4, is a potential source of harm, and on its own it does nothing at all. A foreseeable sequence of events is what turns that hazard into a hazardous situation, which at 3.5 is a circumstance in which people are exposed to the hazard, and only then can harm occur, harm being physical injury or damage to health at 3.3.

The two probabilities in your file sit at two different arrows on that chain. P1 is the probability that the hazardous situation arises, and P2 is the conditional probability that the hazardous situation goes on to lead to the harm, and the product of the two is the probability of occurrence of harm. Annex C.5 of the standard and Clause 5.5 of ISO/TR 24971 both describe that decomposition.

So now you may be asking, why do we bother separating the two? Because each half is defended with completely different evidence. P1 is engineering and field data, meaning failure rates, bench test results, field return rates, use frequency and exposure in the intended environment, and observed rates out of complaints and service data. P2 is clinical, meaning who is exposed and for how long, whether the harm is detectable before it becomes harm, whether it is reversible, and whether anyone intervenes in time.

Most files that combine the two into a single number cannot defend either half, and a reviewer who asks which half of this number is clinical gets silence. The rule that follows is one row per hazardous situation, and not one row per hazard. A single hazard can reach more than one hazardous situation by more than one sequence of events, and each of those carries its own probability, which is why collapsing several sequences into one row makes a probability estimate impossible to defend.

What the Row Should Have Been

The room's rebuilt rows can now be held against the model answer, and the key move in it is that one line about the pumping mechanism becomes 3 rows, because there are 3 distinct hazardous situations reached by 3 distinct sequences, and their severities and their probabilities are genuinely different.

I want to walk the first row as the exemplar. The hazard is stored mechanical energy in the compliant fluid path, recorded here as mechanical energy leading to unintended delivery, and the sequence is that a downstream occlusion raises the pressure in the compliant set, the occlusion then clears, and the stored volume is released before the pump can decompress the line. The hazardous situation is that the patient receives a bolus above the prescribed rate, and the harm is over-infusion, presenting as hypotension and respiratory depression. P1 is a 4, which comes from the complaint trend, P2 is a 4, which comes from the clinical argument about an unattended patient in the home, and severity is a 4.

The P1 versus P2 point becomes concrete if you hold the third row against that one. The hazard category is the same, and yet the third row's under-infusion carries a lower severity and a different P2, because loss of therapy is usually detectable and recoverable, whereas the bolus is neither. That third row is a plunger seal that creeps so that delivered volume drifts below the set rate inside the alarm window, undetected. The second row is a drive train worn beyond its qualified cycle count, where the pump stalls, the stall is not detected by the motion sensor, and therapy stops without the user being alerted.

Then we have the links across to the MDR. This device supplies substances to the patient, so Annex I point 21 is directly on point, and specifically 21.1 requires that the amount delivered is set and maintained accurately, while 21.2 requires means of preventing or indicating inadequacies in the amount delivered. Most files cite point 4 and stop there, and citing 21.1 and 21.2 is what tells a reviewer you have read Chapter II.

The wording at the bottom is what a reviewer would write against the original row. The file does not identify the foreseeable sequences by which the hazard leads to a hazardous situation and does not distinguish that situation from the hazard or the harm, which is 5.4, and no basis is recorded for the probability estimate, which is 5.5. The stated control, "mitigated by design", does not identify a risk control measure at all, and it is not traceable to a verification of implementation or of effectiveness, which is 7.2. No residual risk has been evaluated, which is 7.3, and the row is linked to no general safety and performance requirement at all.


SLIDE 20

The Four Things the Room Reliably Gets Wrong

These are the recurring patterns, and they are the 4 mistakes this exercise produces every time I run it, so naming them is what makes the lesson stick beyond today. The first one I want to name is rebuilding one row instead of several, because the hazard reached more than one hazardous situation, and if your rebuild has a single row in it then you have renamed the problem rather than analyzed it.

Writing a sequence that is only one step long. "Pump fails" is a state and it is not a sequence, because a sequence has a first event, an intermediate condition and an exposure, which is why 5.4 asks for sequences, plural, and combinations of events.

Assigning P1 and P2 without separating the evidence behind them. If you cannot say which half of the number is engineering and which half is clinical, then what you have is a single number that cannot be defended on either basis.

The fourth one is the subtle one and it is worth 20 seconds. Manufacturers write the harm as the clinical event that would be reported rather than as the harm the patient experiences, and a harm, at 3.3, is physical injury or damage to health. The words reportable incident, complaint and recall all name consequences of harm, and none of them is a harm. A reportable event under Medical Device Reporting is not a harm, while hypotension requiring intervention is.


SLIDE 21

Risk Control and Residual Risk

So now that we have established the 4 things the room reliably gets wrong, we can move on to risk control and residual risk, and this is where we will spend the next 13 minutes. Everything in this section sits inside Clause 7 and Clause 8, and Clause 7 on its own runs to 6 parts. It is critical that manufacturers recognize that 7.3, 7.4 and Clause 8 are three different questions, because files routinely answer all three of them as one, and that single confusion is the most valuable thing this section has to offer.


SLIDE 22

The Order of Priority Is an Order, Not a Menu

Both ISO 14971 and the EU Medical Device Regulation say the same thing about risk control, and both of them say it as a sequence rather than as a set of alternatives. Clause 7.1 and MDR Annex I point 4 each give 3 options in a fixed order, and I want to highlight the specific wording of point 4, because it leaves very little room: "In selecting the most appropriate solutions, manufacturers shall, in the following order of priority". You will note that the regulation uses the word shall, and that it sets the options out in a fixed order.

The 3 options, in the order the regulation sets them out, are the following:

1. Inherent Safety by Design: Eliminate the hazard outright, or eliminate the sequence of events that reaches it. This is the category that changes P1, the probability that the hazardous situation arises in the first place.

2. Protective Measures in the Device Itself or in Manufacture: Guards, interlocks, alarms, and detection all sit in this category. These usually change P1, and sometimes they change P2 instead.

3. Information for Safety, and Training: Warnings, precautions, contra-indications, the instructions for use, or IFU, and user training all sit here, and this category rarely changes P1 at all.

The obligation that this order creates is a documentation obligation. Manufacturers do not have to use option 1. They have to be able to show that option 1 was considered, and to record why it was not practicable, and the same holds before the file settles at option 3. A file full of category 3 controls with no option analysis behind them is the commonest Clause 7.1 finding there is, and under MDR point 2 that record cannot rest on cost.

You will note that point 4 requires something before the list even begins, which is that the residual risk associated with each individual hazard and the overall residual risk are both judged acceptable. Both of those determinations are required, and they are required separately.

Then there is the sting in the tail of point 4, which manufacturers forget because it sits after the list rather than inside it: "Manufacturers shall inform users of any residual risks." That obligation reaches any residual risk and not only the significant ones, which makes it a broader disclosure obligation than the one in ISO 14971 Clause 8, where the standard speaks of significant residual risks in the accompanying documentation.

The last point here is one I want to make firmly. Information for safety does not reduce the probability that the hazardous situation arises. A warning cannot stop an occlusion forming. It may reduce P2, if it changes what the user does in time, and that is a legitimate claim. However, if your file shows a category 3 control dropping P1 by 3 bands, that is the row a reviewer will open first.


SLIDE 23

Clause 7.2 Asks for Two Verifications

If you take one slide home from today, take this one. Clause 7.2 requires verification of the implementation of each risk control measure, and it separately requires verification of the effectiveness of each risk control measure. That is 2 verifications, 2 records, and 2 genuinely different kinds of evidence.

Implementation answers a narrow question, which is whether the control is actually in the product as it was designed. A drawing revision, a build record, an incoming inspection result, or a code review carries that.

Effectiveness answers a much harder question, which is whether the control does the job it was credited with doing in the risk estimate. It is the harder question, it is also the one that gets skipped, because the answer has to connect back to the number you wrote in the residual risk column. If you credited a control with dropping P1 from 4 to 2, your effectiveness record has to support a factor-of-a-hundred claim.

The table sets out the pattern for each control category. For inherent safety by design, implementation is the drawing or specification revision, the design verification report, and the build records showing that the change is in the product, while effectiveness is a test showing that the hazardous situation can no longer arise, or that it arises at the claimed lower rate, together with field data after the change. For a protective measure, implementation is the specification and design verification of the guard, interlock, alarm or detector along with the production test records, while effectiveness means challenging the measure under worst-case conditions across the full operating range, not only at nominal, and showing it triggers. For an alarm specifically, implementation is the alarm specification, levels and priorities verified against the design input, while effectiveness is audibility and comprehension in the real use environment with the real user, which means that a bench sound-pressure measurement is implementation and not effectiveness.

Look at the bottom row, because that row is where most files fail. For information for safety, implementation is the IFU or label approval record, the artwork release, and the training material release. Effectiveness is comprehension and behavior. An approved IFU proves implementation and it proves nothing whatsoever about effectiveness, and the only credible effectiveness evidence for a category 3 control is usability evidence that users find it, understand it, and act on it, which is the bridge across to IEC 62366-1.

So now you may be asking where the enforcement of this comes from. FDA reaches it through ISO 13485 Clause 7.3 design verification and validation, while a notified body reaches it through Annex ZA point 4, and both arrive at the same place. The test for any effectiveness record is whether it supports the size of the reduction you claimed in the residual risk column, and if it does not, then your residual estimate is unsupported.


SLIDE 24

7.5 and 7.6, the Two Clauses Procedures Stop Short Of

Clause 7.5 requires manufacturers to review the effects of the risk control measures themselves, and it asks for a review against 2 things: any new hazards or hazardous situations that the controls have introduced, and whether the estimated risks for previously identified hazardous situations are affected. Any new or increased risk is then managed in accordance with 5.5 through 7.4, which means it goes back through estimation, evaluation, control, and residual risk evaluation. It gets its own row.

The examples down the left of the slide are all real, and they make the clause concrete rather than procedural, and they include: the escalating alarm that solves audibility and creates alarm fatigue, the plasticizer-free set, otherwise described as the DEHP-free set, that solves leachables and changes stiffness and therefore pumping accuracy, and the stronger pouch seal that solves sterility and makes aseptic opening harder.

Clause 7.6 is the completeness step, and it asks manufacturers to confirm that the risks from all identified hazardous situations have been considered. This is where the routing out of Clause 6 matters, and it is the part manufacturers miss. An acceptable risk goes straight to 7.6, and its estimated risk is treated as the residual risk. So 7.6 applies to every hazardous situation in your file, including all of the ones that never needed a control in the first place, and a procedure that ends at 7.5 has no completeness step at all. Manufacturers assume the acceptable rows drop out of the process, and they do not.

Check your own procedure tonight and ask whether it has a step for 7.6, because Clause 7 runs to 7.6, not to 7.5, and if it doesn't, that is a gap you can close this month. The Excel toolkit flags both of these for you: "New risks not assessed (Clause 7.5)" on the Risk Controls sheet, and a completeness check on every hazard row.


SLIDE 25

7.3, 7.4 and 8 Are Three Different Questions

This slide is the conceptual center of the section: three questions, three records, and three answers that get collapsed into one.

7.3 is asked per risk. Is this residual risk acceptable against the criteria that the plan declared? If the answer is yes, you are done and you move on. If the answer is no, you go on to 7.4.

7.4 is also asked per risk, but it is conditional, and it applies only where the residual risk is not acceptable and further risk control is not practicable. Where both of those gates are met, manufacturers gather and review the data and literature in order to decide whether the benefits of the intended use outweigh that residual risk. It is important to note that both gates have to be met, and not just one of them. A benefit-risk analysis used as a shortcut around risk control will itself be written up as a finding.

Clause 8 is not asked per risk at all, because it is asked per device. It asks whether the overall residual risk of the device is acceptable, by the method the plan declared and against the criteria the plan declared. Several individually acceptable risks can be collectively unacceptable, and that is especially true where those risks share a single common cause, or where the safety of the device rests on a stack of warnings.

The toolkit's Residual Risk sheet lists the inputs that a credible Clause 8 evaluation considers, and they include:

- The combination of individual residual risks, particularly where several of them affect the same patient episode
- Risks that share a single common cause
- Conflicting requirements between risk controls
- The number and nature of the warnings that safety rests on
- Comparison with similar devices and the state of the art
- The clinical benefit and the benefit-risk conclusion
- Information from production and post-production
- Disclosure of significant residual risks in the accompanying information
- The conclusion itself, recorded against the criteria the plan declared and signed by someone who holds the authority to sign it

If Clause 8 in your file is a single sentence saying that "the overall residual risk is acceptable" sitting underneath a table of green cells, then you don't have a Clause 8 evaluation at all. What you have is a roll-up of the rows above it.

How a Reviewer Actually Walks a Risk File

You have now been the reviewer for 14 minutes, so the moves I sketched at the start of the session can be named properly, and there are 6 of them. What a reviewer does with a risk file is not a document review. It is a traversal, in a fixed order, and it starts from the device rather than from the file. This is also the slide people tend to photograph.

The first move is that they pick the hazard, and you do not. They pick it from the device's clinical profile, from your own complaint data, or from the recall history of devices like yours, and never from your hazard index. Then they walk that one row end to end, and if the file holds for a row picked at random, they will believe the rest of them. If it breaks, they pick 3 more.

The second move is to the estimate, where they ask for the basis. Where did P1 come from, what population, over what period, and out of which record? "Engineering judgement" ends that conversation badly.

The third move is to the control, where they read the option analysis. Was the order of priority worked, and is there a record of why a higher option was not practicable, on a non-economic basis? The fourth move is to ask for the effectiveness record instead of the implementation record, which is really a question of whether the evidence supports the size of the reduction claimed, and it is where most traversals stop.

The fifth move is out to the labeling and the clinical evaluation. Is the residual risk disclosed, and do the criteria in the risk file and the benefit-risk parameters in the clinical evaluation plan agree? The sixth and last move is out to the post-market data and back. What has come in since, did any of it change a probability, a severity, or the state of the art, and can you show the file revision it produced?

For the documentation expectations behind that traversal, I would point you back to the Team-NB best practice guidance on technical documentation, now at V4, adopted 21 April 2026, which replaced the V3 of 9 April 2025 that most people are still citing. Section 5 is Benefit-Risk Analysis and Risk Management, and section 5.5 is a named list of the common pitfalls in risk management that notified bodies observe. The guidance expects you to state whether your risk management process is based on EN ISO 14971. It expects no acceptance on an RPN alone and no green zone, each risk decided individually against predefined criteria, a statement that the clinical benefits outweigh all of the residual risks, and 3 distinct risk assessments covering design, production and process, and clinical or application, which catches the firms that have only ever done the design one.

What V4 added is precisely the finding this room has just written: a pitfall for a warning or a caution in the instructions for use offered as a risk control with no usability engineering evidence of its effectiveness. A notified body association wrote that down as a named pitfall in April 2026, so if your file does that, it is not an obscure risk that you have been unlucky to be caught on. It is on their list.

SLIDE 32

Clause 10 and the Four Questions

Clause 10 has 4 subclauses, and each does a different job: 10.1 is general, 10.2 is information collection, 10.3 is information review, and 10.4 is actions. The risk management plan has to name these activities under clause 4.4 g), which means the plan is the first place a reviewer looks to see whether the manufacturer ever intended to do any of this. This is the clause most often found completely empty on a device that has been marketed for years.

10.2 is the list of collection sources, and manufacturers are required to collect from each of them. Those sources include: information generated during production and monitoring of the production process, information from the user, information from installation, servicing and maintenance, publicly available information, and information on the generally acknowledged state of the art. That last one is the source nobody collects, and because it is named alongside the others, its absence is visible to anybody reading your procedure. It should be added that this is a general list, and it is not a complete description of every channel your own device generates.

10.3 is the heart of the clause, and for every piece of safety-relevant information that arrives, manufacturers ask 4 questions and record the answers. There are 4 of them rather than one general review because each one carries a different consequence, and the consequence is the part the file has to show.

The First Question is Whether There is a Previously Unrecognized Hazard or Hazardous Situation
If the answer is yes, that is a new row, and the row earns full estimation, evaluation, risk control and residual risk evaluation under clauses 5.5 to 7.4.

The Next Question is Whether an Estimated Risk is No Longer Acceptable
Risk control is reopened, and both the residual risk for that hazardous situation and the overall residual risk are revisited, which is the step teams skip when they update one row and stop.

The Third Question is Whether the Original Risk Estimate is No Longer Valid
The estimate is corrected and the basis for it is updated, and this is the question that catches the optimistic P1 values that went in when nobody had data.

Finally the Last Question is Whether the State of the Art Has Changed
Your AFAP position may have moved without anything having failed, and comparator devices, standards and guidance documents all count as evidence that it moved.

10.4 tells you what happens when any answer is yes: the impact is evaluated as an input to the risk management process, the risk management file is reviewed, and if the residual risk has increased or is no longer acceptable, the impact of previously implemented controls is evaluated and fed into the risk management review.

The practical test of whether this works at your firm is one you can run on your own file tonight. Pick one complaint from 18 months ago and try to find the risk file revision it produced. If there is no revision, and no recorded decision that none was needed, then the loop is open.


SLIDE 33

The MDR Plumbing That Clause 10 Has to Connect To

In the European Union, Clause 10 is not free-standing. It plugs into a named set of obligations under the Medical Device Regulation, or MDR, and in practice the notified body checks the joints rather than the pipes.

Article 10(2) is the headline, and it is one sentence: manufacturers shall establish, document, implement and maintain a system for risk management as described in Section 3 of Annex I. Article 10(9)(e) then puts risk management inside the quality management system as a named aspect it has to address, which is how it becomes auditable rather than merely required.

Annex I point 3, sub-points (e) and (f), is the loop written into the law itself. Manufacturers are required to evaluate the impact of information from production and from post-market surveillance on hazards, on the frequency of their occurrence, on risk estimates, on the overall risk, on the benefit-risk ratio and on risk acceptability, and then to amend the control measures where that evaluation tells them to.

Article 83(3)(a) makes updating the benefit-risk determination and improving risk management the first named use of post-market surveillance data, and Article 83(3) closes with "the technical documentation shall be updated accordingly." Annex II point 5 is where that lands, because the technical documentation has to contain the benefit-risk analysis in Sections 1 and 8 of Annex I, together with the solutions adopted and the results of the risk management under Section 3.

Annex III point 1(b) is the single most audited joint in the whole set, because the post-market surveillance plan must contain suitable indicators and threshold values used in the continuous reassessment of the benefit-risk analysis and of the risk management. Indicators and thresholds are quantitative, so if your surveillance plan has no numbers in it, that is a finding waiting to be written.

Three more provisions run through the same evidence. Article 86(1)(a) requires the PSUR to set out the conclusions of the benefit-risk determination throughout the lifetime of the device. Article 88(1) requires trend reporting where a statistically significant increase would change the benefit-risk analysis in Sections 1 and 8 of Annex I. Article 61(10) provides that where conformity based on clinical data is not appropriate, the justification rests on the results of the risk management, which is a heavier load than most files are built to carry.

Annex XIV Part B point 8 then sends the conclusions of the post-market clinical follow-up evaluation report back into both the clinical evaluation and the risk management. Recital 33 states that the risk management and clinical evaluation processes should be inter-dependent and regularly updated, which is what a reviewer cites when they ask why your risk file and your clinical evaluation report disagree.

There are 2 numbering traps here that will affect your cross-references. Corrigendum C2 renumbered Annex III from 1.1 and 1.2 to 1 and 2, so an SOP citing "Annex III Section 1.1" is citing superseded numbering. Additionally, Annex XIV Part B point 6.1(d) still refers to the benefit-risk ratio in Sections 1 and 9 of Annex I. Section 9 is the Annex XVI provision on devices without an intended medical purpose, and the benefit-risk sections are 1 and 8. That cross-reference was never corrected, unlike the identical error in Article 88(1), which was.

10. Rebuild the GSPR matrix against Annex I as it reads now, and not against the 7 deviations from 2012.

Remember the hands that stayed up at the start of this session, when I asked whose finding was verification of effectiveness. That is why, of these 10 items, the one I would address first is number two. It is the finding that appears most often in the files I review, and it is also the cheapest one to close, because the work has usually already been done and it simply was not written down anywhere as effectiveness.


SLIDE 38

Sources, and What to Re-Verify

I am not going to read this list to you, and the handout carries all of it in full with the Official Journal references, split into the standards, the EU law, the US law, and the guidance and position papers, which are labeled as secondary because that is what they are.

What I do want to point at is the band across the bottom. This material was verified in September 2026. The facilitator guide therefore carries a pre-delivery accuracy checklist, and that checklist needs to be re-run against the primary sources before anyone presents this material or relies on a citation out of it.


SLIDE 39

A Risk File Is an Argument, Not an Archive

If you take one idea out of today, take this one. A risk file is an argument, and not an archive. Every number in it has a basis, every control has 2 verifications, every unacceptable residual risk has a benefit-risk record, and every post-production input has a recorded answer to 4 questions. If any one of those is missing, the file does not hold as an argument, and that is precisely the row a reviewer pulls. It is important to note that everything today is workshop material, and it is not legal or regulatory advice for your specific device.

Before I open it up, there are 3 questions that come at the end of every delivery of this workshop, so I want to answer them now.

The first is always whether you have to use ISO 14971 at all. The answer is not as law, in either jurisdiction, and that is the whole of the two-regulator section, with the two-column mapping sitting in your handout. What matters is the last clause of that answer, which is that the activity is inspected regardless of which standard you name.

The next question is whether you can keep your three-band matrix. You can, but be ready to say which clause the middle band implements, because ISO 14971:2019 does not have one, and under point 2 of Annex I an amber "acceptable with justification" band does not create a stopping point that cost can justify.

The last one is how many rows a risk file should have, and that is the wrong question. It is 1 row per hazardous situation, which is determined by the device and not by a target. However, a file with fewer rows than the device has failure modes has collapsed the analysis somewhere, and that collapse is findable.

If we have time once the questions are done, I am happy to put one of your own rows up on the screen and walk it end to end, which is usually the most useful 10 minutes of the session.

So with that said, it is easy to recognize why it is so critical to maintain a traceable and defensible risk management file for manufacturers who intend to defend their risk management before a notified body or an FDA investigator.
