# Speaker style specification

Derived by four independent analysts from five of the speaker's own verbatim-read
transcripts, then synthesised. This is the contract the transcript drafters wrote to.

STYLE SPECIFICATION — Jordan Elder, verbatim-read presentation transcripts

OPENING FORMULA (reproduce near-verbatim; four moves, no deviation)

Move 1, greeting: "Hello and thank you Laura for that introduction and thank you to those who are joining us for today's presentation."

Move 2, self-naming plus topic: "My name is Jordan Elder and today we are going to discuss [TOPIC — the full talk title, in title case, no colon subtitle]."

Move 3, scope preview: two to five sentences, each beginning with one of "We will discuss… / We will also identify… / We will explore… / We will delve into… / This session will highlight… / The presentation will emphasize… / Finally, we will discuss…". Name the actual sub-topics in a serial-comma string. State any exclusion here in one sentence ("Although several PMA elements may apply to this presentation, Class III devices as a whole are excluded from the scope of this discussion.").

Move 4, handoff: "So with that said, lets dive in." Note "lets" without an apostrophe — keep it. Substitute a slide-pointing sentence for Move 4 only when the first slide shows a figure ("As you can see from the product roadmap, the regulatory process begins early and should not be overlooked.").

Never precede Move 1 with a hook, statistic, anecdote, or scene-setting line.

CLOSING FORMULA (reproduce verbatim)

Optional hinge sentence first: "So with that said, [one sentence stating why the material matters — e.g. it is easy to recognize why it is so critical to understand these distinctions for manufacturers who intend to navigate the 510(k) process successfully]."

Then, exactly: "This concludes the presentation portion of this discussion. I want to thank each of you who attended, and I welcome any questions you may have at this time."

RULES

1. Mark slide boundaries with a bare all-caps scaffolding line on its own — "SLIDE 1", "SLIDE 2", "INTRO", "Conclusion". Never write "Slide 3:" inside prose and never number slides inside a sentence.
2. Where a slide carries a topic name, put it on its own line as a bare title-case noun phrase ("Data Provenance", "Validation Strategies and Performance Assessment") and start the prose on the next line on a new idea. Never restate the heading as a spoken sentence.
3. Open roughly two thirds of slides cold with a declarative claim about the regulatory landscape, preferably a subordinate or nominalised opener: "In order to…", "After you have…", "While high-quality data is essential…", "Demonstrating substantial equivalence… requires…", "Maintaining X throughout the Y lifecycle is…".
4. Open a slide that follows a conceptual slide with the recap template: "So now that we have [a baseline understanding of / established] X, we can [move on to / begin to look at] Y." Reuse it word-for-word at two or three boundaries in the same talk rather than varying it.
5. Voice the audience's question before answering it, at a slide opening or a mid-slide pivot only: "Some of you may be asking what X is?" / "So now you may be asking, how does FDA…?" / a fully ventriloquised line ("Alright great, so you told me about the submission pathways… but how do I determine what is needed for my specific product?"). Answer in the very next sentence. Never end a slide on a question.
6. Signpost agenda moves in the first person as standalone sentences: "I would like to take a moment to discuss a common scenario." / "I want to highlight…" / "Before we get too deep into X, I want to highlight Y."
7. Introduce the mechanism that solves a problem with "This is where…": "This is where we would turn to the FDA presubmission process."
8. Average ~21 words per sentence, median 20. Put about two thirds of sentences in the 11-30-word range, let roughly one in six run past 30, and keep sub-10-word sentences to about one in seven.
9. Extend a long sentence with a comma plus non-restrictive ", which …" rather than starting a new sentence. This is the signature joining device — use it several times per talk. Also allow bare restrictive "which" where an editor would write "that" ("a predicate device which has previously received FDA clearance").
10. Use "while" mid-sentence as a concessive ("…, while lowering the overall patient risk, …") and as a sentence-opening "While …," clause.
11. Put "However," at the head of its own sentence by default. Use "; however," inside a sentence only in a two-clause grant-then-override. Semicolons otherwise appear about once per talk.
12. Build paragraphs of 3 sentences and roughly 65 words. Keep 1-4 sentences for about 85% of paragraphs. Never write an 8-10 sentence block.
13. Always precede a list with a colon-terminated lead-in built on "include:", "may include:", or "the following …:". Never drop a list in without a colon.
14. Render numbered items as a title-case gerund or noun label, a colon, then one to three full sentences: "1. Determining Device Classification: Manufacturers must accurately identify their device's classification…".
15. Do not enforce parallelism in bullets. Mix bare noun-phrase fragments with no terminal period ("Planned Modifications") and full second-person sentences with periods, sometimes two per bullet. Where two bullets form a conjoined condition, end the first with a capitalised "AND".
16. Never speak a list number aloud. When sequence must be heard, use a standalone headline sentence with a non-parallel ordinal: "The First Element is the Description of the Modification" / "The Next Element is the Modification Protocol" / "The Third and Oftentimes Overlooked Element is the Traceability Table" / "Finally the Last Element is the overall Impact Assessment".
17. For a problem/solution deck, use the three inline labels "Challenge:", "Common Mistake:", "Best Practice:" — "Challenge:" inline with its first sentence, the other two alone on a line with prose beneath. Alternate one Challenge-plus-Common-Mistake slide with one Best-Practice slide.
18. For a misconceptions section, number "Myth:" lines with an indented "- Reality:" answer, voicing the myth in the audience's first person ("Myth: Small changes to my cleared device don't need a new 510(k)."). Introduce the section with a credibility frame and "So let's start with our first Myth."
19. For an enumerated pitfalls deck, head each item "Challenge #N: " plus a gerund-negative noun phrase ("Not providing sufficient documentation."), then a short paragraph.
20. Announce only the first item of any series ("The first challenge I want to discuss today is:") and then stop announcing. Later items arrive unlabelled.
21. Gloss every acronym on first use, either parenthetically ("Quality Management System (QMS)") or in spoken apposition ("a process called a Predetermined Change Control Plan, or PCCP" / "otherwise known as a pre-sub"). Re-gloss the same acronym later in a long deck rather than assuming it stuck. Never re-expand mid-paragraph after the gloss.
22. Use digits for all quantities, including 1, 2, 5, 7. Give day counts as bare numerals plus a qualifier ("70 days", "90 review days", "180 calendar days"). Spell out a quantity only in a soft range ("three to eight months").
23. Front every statistic with a year or period and a hedge: "In 2024, only 84% of all devices received a substantial equivalence decision…". Follow the headline figure with its complement in the next sentence ("The remaining 16% either were…"). Use % and $ with comma separators.
24. Cite regulation as a bare section number behind a framing phrase ("Stipulated through federal regulation 21 CFR 807.87", "specifically under section 876.1500", "As required under sections 807.92"). Cite guidance by quoted title inside "(e.g., …)". Cite flowchart nodes by raw label ("B3, B3.1 and B3.2").
25. Say "clearance" and "cleared," never "approval," for 510(k) devices, and call the distinction out explicitly as a teaching point. "Approved" appears only inside scare quotes as the error being corrected.
26. Keep FDA third person and agentive — "FDA requires", "FDA recommends", "FDA expects", "FDA has indicated". Use "the agency" only as a second reference after FDA has just been named. Never editorialise about whether a requirement is reasonable.
27. Allocate pronouns by role: "we" for the shared analytic walk ("we turn to a few different areas"); "you / your" for the listener's own device, submission and obligations; "manufacturers" (third person) for industry-wide obligations and for all failures; "I" only for the intro, agenda moves, war stories with a number attached, own-judgement advice, and the thanks.
28. Split advice by source. Bare imperatives carry best practice ("Don't make this selection in isolation. Leverage the FDA's Q-Submission program…"). "FDA recommends / it is highly recommended" carries anything the agency said. "I always recommend / I will always caution" is reserved for uncited judgement calls.
29. Ration first-person anecdotes to two or three per deck, clustered in one late segment, never in the technical body. Frame each with "I actually recently worked with a manufacturer who…" or "I once worked with a startup who…", keep it to one or two sentences, name no client or device, and end on the transferable lesson. Back a credibility claim with a number and a published statistic immediately after.
30. Fence every list and generalisation immediately after it, not in an end-of-deck disclaimer: "It is important to note that this list is a general list, and each device will have specific documentation and testing requirements." / "This is by no means an exhaustive list…". Use "may or may not" for anything genuinely optional.
31. Carry all visual cues inside spoken prose and make them concrete: "As you can see from the product roadmap", "You will note below that", "I would like to apologize for the overall look of this flowchart, I recognize it is messy; however, this was taken directly from FDA's guidance document." Write no bracketed stage directions of any kind.
32. Use the serial comma without exception, and let coordinate strings of three to six nouns or gerunds carry the content load ("data collection, curation, annotation, and ongoing monitoring").
33. Put regulatory terms of art in double quotes ("substantially equivalent", "FDA Approved", "stacking") and coined or flagged concepts in single quotes ('guardrails', 'predicate device').
34. Vary register by section, not within it: contractions ("don't", "it's", "you'll", "isn't") cluster in advice and anecdote passages; formal expository slides stay uncontracted. Target roughly 20-25 contractions per 11,000 words.
35. Prefer a spaced en dash " – " for a mid-sentence aside; reserve parentheses for acronym glosses, "(e.g., …)" and short clarifications. At most a handful of dashes in a whole talk.

STOCK-PHRASE INVENTORY AND DOSAGE

Load-bearing, must appear: the opening four moves and the closing two sentences (verbatim, once each). "It is important to note that…" — 3 to 5 times per talk, varying the verb across note / know / remember / highlight. "So now that we have… we can…" — 2 to 3 times. "So with that said," — 2 times maximum, once as the Move-4 handoff and once before the sign-off. Colon-terminated list lead-ins with "include:" — as often as there are lists.

Use once per talk, no more: "It should be noted that…" (facts that narrow a claim, as distinct from things the audience must act on); "It is critical that…" (compliance risk only); "This is where…"; "I would like to take a moment to…"; "Some of you may be asking…"; "In regards to" (his consistent usage — never "with regard to"); "In essence" or "In conclusion" as a section cap; "Ultimately, …" as a final sentence.

Use at most once across two talks, or they become parody: "Trust me when I say", "I cannot stress how vital", "non-negotiable" (data/security governance only), "It is imperative that", "Alright", "Alright great, so", "Well,", "So first off", "Fortunately for us", the 'guardrails' passage, "playbook", "uphill battle", "baked right into", "read between the lines", "backed into a corner", "a great pulse of".

Adjective stack to draw on freely, roughly one per paragraph, not two: critical, crucial, essential, robust, comprehensive, appropriate, significant, rigorous, meticulous, proactively. Connective set, and nothing outside it: However, Additionally, Furthermore, Finally, Therefore, In essence, So.

WORDS PER SLIDE

Measured across 36 slide sections: median ~180 spoken words; mean ~215. Target 150-270 words for a standard content slide. A tight survey deck runs 119-179 words per slide with very low variance (aim 150 and hold it). A dense definitional or requirements slide runs 250-400, and a slide carrying a full multi-part deliverable (a PCCP's four elements, a myths block) runs 500-1,000 and should be written as a stack of 3-sentence paragraphs with bare headings rather than one long block. A purely transitional slide — a forward reference, a figure pointer — runs 60-90 words and is a single short paragraph. The intro block is ~90-200 words; a labelled Conclusion is two flowing prose paragraphs, ~200-250 words.

SENTENCE RHYTHM

The default shape is a nominalised or subordinate opener, a main clause carrying the obligation, and a ", which …" tail that states the consequence. Long sentences are extended, not stacked. Model sentences, written for this spec:

"Establishing a clear validation strategy early in development is essential for AI-enabled devices, which allows manufacturers to align their testing with FDA expectations before resources have been committed to the wrong studies."

"In order to support a claim of substantial equivalence, manufacturers must document the intended use, the technological characteristics, and the performance testing for each proposed configuration, and it is important to note that this documentation is device-specific."

"While a Pre-Submission is entirely voluntary, FDA has up to 70 days to provide written feedback, which in practice means that manufacturers who engage the agency early often recover that time during review."

LEVEL OF POLISH

The corpus is first-draft spoken prose, read aloud rather than copy-edited. It contains roughly one visible slip every few paragraphs: doubled words, homophone typos, missing apostrophes, subject-verb agreement failures, half-revised clauses, and inconsistent casing of the same term (eSTAR / e-STAR, Pre-Sub / pre-sub / presubmission, single-use / single use). Write competent professional prose with the looseness of rhythm that comes from drafting for the ear — long additive sentences, the occasional comma splice in a conversational or reassurance sentence, a term styled two ways in one deck, and no smoothing of the repeated stock phrases. Do not introduce errors deliberately. A flawlessly copy-edited, uniformly polished, perfectly parallel draft reads as off-fingerprint; the fix is looser rhythm and honest repetition, not planted typos.

ANTI-PATTERNS

Never compress the note-taking frame: no "Note that", "Notably", "Importantly", "Keep in mind that". Never use the bookish LLM connectives: no "Moreover", "Thus", "Hence", "Firstly / Secondly / Thirdly", "Lastly" as a bare chain, "In summary", "Nevertheless", "Consequently". Never write "shall" — obligation is "must", "are required to", "needs to". Never write "i.e."; "e.g." always takes its comma.

Never use the LLM transition set: no "Let's dive into", "Let's unpack that" as a standalone line, "But here's the thing", "Why does this matter?", "The answer is simple", "Now, you might be wondering", "Moving on", "With that, let's turn to".

No short punchy fragments for effect ("The result? Delays."), no one-word sentences, no sentence-initial "And yet". No rhetorical triples for rhythm. No paired em dashes as a tic. No markdown emphasis in prose — no bold, no italics, no inline headers; section heads are bare title-case lines. No emoji. No exclamation marks anywhere. No bulleted "Key Takeaways" or "Summary" close.

No bracketed stage directions ("[pause]", "(next slide)") and no generic pointers ("on this slide", "as you can see on the slide"). No audience interaction — no polls, no show of hands, no "drop a question in the chat". No casual address — no "folks", "guys", "everyone", "friends". No second-person coaching call to action at a section end ("Take this back to your team", "Remember three things"); advice lives inside body prose addressed to "manufacturers" or "you" as the submitter.

No first-person opinion hedges: no "I think", "I believe", "in my opinion", "frankly", "to be honest", "let me be clear". No enthusiasm register: no "awesome", "amazing", "huge", "game-changer", "obviously", "basically", "deep dive", "at the end of the day", "bottom line". No vague placeholder nouns — no "things", no "stuff". No unsourced magnitude claims; every number carries a year, a percentage, a day count, a dollar figure, or a CFR section. No spelled-out small numbers in figures-eligible positions. No "the regulator" and no "the Agency" as a first reference. No criticism of FDA in the speaker's own voice. No named client, startup, device or brand in an anecdote, and no hypothetical framing ("imagine a company that…"). No extended metaphor or analogy. No promise of outcome — the strongest available form is "can significantly improve their chances of a successful 510(k) submission". No varying the opening and closing formulas for freshness, and no naming the introducer again after the first sentence.
