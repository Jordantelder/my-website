For 20 years most of us prepared for audits, and the calendar told us when to start. That model is gone. SLIDE 5

The first of those 3 areas is who can arrive at your site without warning, and it is a significantly longer list than most manufacturers assume.

The PIP breast-implant scandal exposed how little a scheduled surveillance audit could actually see, which led the European Commission to issue Recommendation 2013/473/EU, asking notified bodies to audit unannounced at least once every 3 years. The MDR, which is the European Union's Medical Device Regulation, then wrote unannounced audits into law under Annex IX, section 3.4, with a minimum of at least once every 5 years per manufacturer and an explicit right to extend those visits to critical suppliers and subcontractors. The notified body must keep a plan for these audits and must not disclose that plan to you, which means there is no version of this process where you negotiate the date, and the same expectation applies under the IVDR, the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation, for those of you on the diagnostics side.

In regards to FDA, domestic routine and for-cause inspections have always been unannounced, and the agency's inspection authority sits in section 704 of the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. What changed in 2025 is the announced expansion of unannounced inspections at foreign sites, which means the old assumption that "overseas means pre-announced" no longer holds for anyone. The QMSR, which is the Quality Management System Regulation at 21 CFR 820, has also been in force since 2 Feb 2026.

MDSAP, the Medical Device Single Audit Program, covers Australia, Brazil, Canada, Japan, and the United States, and those audits are normally announced and run on a 3-year cycle made up of an initial certification audit, 2 surveillance audits, and a recertification audit. However, the program does allow special and unannounced audits when a regulator requests one or when serious signals appear, so announced is the normal case and not a guarantee.

The fourth row is competent authorities and other regulators, and it includes the EU member-state authorities, ANVISA in Brazil, the TGA in Australia, and the PMDA in Japan. Notice from these bodies varies, and for-cause visits are often unannounced, which are typically driven by market surveillance activity, vigilance signals, complaints, or recalls. The last row is the one manufacturers forget, which is customers and distributors, and while those customer quality audits are usually announced and arrive through the quality agreements you have already signed, they are run against the same records an auditor would ask for.

This table is a general one, and the notice you actually receive may or may not match what is written here, which depends on your markets, your certificate scope, and your own history with each of these organizations. The point I want to land before we go further is that the model where a manufacturer prepares for an audit is finished, and the only workable posture left is to be ready every day.


SLIDE 6

The 2026 Regulatory Snapshot

In order to talk about audit readiness in any useful way, we first have to agree on what the regulatory landscape actually looks like in 2026, and there are 6 things I want you to carry in your head for the rest of this session. While all 6 of them will touch your quality system somewhere, the first 2 are where the majority of your readiness effort is going to land.

FDA QMSR

On 2 February 2026, the Quality System Regulation was replaced by the QMSR, which incorporates ISO 13485:2016 into 21 CFR 820 by reference instead of restating the requirements in FDA's own language. On top of that incorporation, FDA has added a small number of agency-specific requirements. The first, at 820.10, carries the general requirements for the quality management system. The second, at 820.35, covers records, including complaint and servicing records and the Unique Device Identification, or UDI, information tied to them. The third, at 820.45, covers labeling and packaging controls.

The Quality System Inspection Technique, otherwise known as QSIT, is retired, which matters to this room because a great many mock audit programs were built around the QSIT subsystems. It is important to note that the old exemption under 820.180(c), which kept management review records, internal audit records, and supplier audit records out of routine FDA review, did not carry over into the new regulation. Plan for investigators to ask for all 3 of those record sets, and plan for them to read what they are given.

EU MDR and IVDR

The extended transition dates under the Medical Device Regulation and the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation keep a large number of legacy devices in scope for years to come. Stipulated through Regulation 2023/607, MDR legacy devices run to the end of 2027 or 2028 depending on the device and the certificate, and under Regulation 2024/1860 the IVDR dates run from 2027 through 2029. However, unannounced audits continue regardless of transition status, under the same Annex IX provision we looked at a moment ago.

Alongside those dates, the duties of the Person Responsible for Regulatory Compliance, or PRRC, your post-market surveillance obligations, and the Periodic Safety Update Report, or PSUR, all remain live, and EUDAMED continues its phased roll-out.

MDSAP

MDSAP gives you one audit that serves 5 regulators, across the same 5 countries I named a moment ago, further jurisdictions participate as affiliates, and it is mandatory for Health Canada Class II through Class IV licences. Nonconformities are graded 1 through 5, and grades 4 and 5 are escalated to the participating regulators within 5 business days, which means a finding can be sitting with a regulator before your team has finished drafting a response.

United Kingdom

UK MDR 2002, as amended, remains the operative regulation in the United Kingdom, and CE marking is accepted there to 30 June 2028 for certificates issued under the old directives, the MDD, the AIMDD, or the IVDD, and to 30 June 2030 for certificates issued under the MDR or the IVDR. New post-market surveillance regulations have been in force since 16 June 2025, and manufacturers based outside the United Kingdom are required to appoint a UK Responsible Person.

ISO 13485 Itself

The standard sitting underneath all of this is still the 2016 edition, together with Amendment 1 from 2024, which adds climate change considerations, and it is the harmonised European version, EN ISO 13485:2016 with the A11 amendment of 2021, that carries the presumption of conformity under the MDR and the IVDR. A revision is under way in ISO/TC 210, so track the draft stages as they publish and expect a transition period rather than an overnight switch. I always recommend building a comprehensive transition plan once the draft stabilizes, instead of waiting for a published standard.

Other Major Markets

The remaining markets most of you deal with include Brazil under RDC 665/2022, the Brazilian Good Manufacturing Practices, Japan under MHLW Ordinance 169, China under NMPA GMP, Korea under KGMP, Saudi Arabia under its own SFDA requirements, and India under MDR 2017. Each of these is largely aligned with ISO 13485, which is the good news, and each of them carries local deltas on registration, records, language, and reporting, which is where the work actually is. Every date on this snapshot is current as of September 2026, and this is an area where dates move, so verify them against the source before you plan around them.


SLIDE 7

What Auditors Actually Test

Whatever badge is on the lanyard, and whatever regime sent them, auditors ask the same 3 questions, and they ask them in the same sequence.

The First Question is Show Me the Procedure.

Here the auditor is establishing whether a documented process exists at all, and then whether that process meets both the standard and the regulations that apply to this device in this market, which is a test that a surprising number of well-written procedures fail on the second half.

The Next Question is Show Me the Records.

This is a different test from the first one, because the auditor is no longer reading your intent, they are looking for proof that the procedure is followed every time and not only on the days when somebody is watching.

The Third Question is Show Me It Happening.

This question is answered on the floor rather than in the conference room. The auditor will walk the production area, interview the operators who do the work, pick a single unit, and follow it, which is the only reliable way to find out whether practice matches paper. Most quality management system failures live in the gap between the second question and the third.

Where Findings Cluster, Year After Year

The areas that account for the bulk of findings include: corrective and preventive action, or CAPA, and specifically root cause and effectiveness under 8.5.2, complaint handling and reporting to regulators under 8.2.2 and 8.2.3, purchasing and supplier controls under 7.4, and process validation and revalidation under 7.5.6. The list continues with design controls and design changes under 7.3, nonconforming product and rework under 8.3, document and record control under 4.2.4 and 4.2.5, management review effectiveness under 5.6, and competence, training, and the effectiveness of that training under 6.2.

Whether you are reading FDA 483 observation data, notified body nonconformity summaries, or MDSAP audit reports, the order of that list shifts from year to year and the membership of it does not, and CAPA and complaint handling have led it for more than a decade. This is a pattern rather than a prediction, and your own audit and complaint history will tell you more about where your particular exposure sits than any published summary will.

Every regime we have just looked at either adopts ISO 13485 outright or sits directly on top of it, which makes the standard the backbone of readiness rather than one more document in the stack. So now that we have a baseline understanding of who can arrive and what they are empowered to ask for, we can move on to the clauses those auditors work through, and to the chain of evidence that has to sit behind each one.


SLIDE 9

ISO 13485 as the Backbone

Before we get too deep into the audit-critical sub-clauses, I want to put the same map in front of everyone, because this is a mixed room. Some of you live inside this standard every day, and for the rest of you, you'll find the case studies later land far better with this structure in view.

Quality Management System

Clause 4 requires a risk-based approach to the processes of the system under 4.1, control of outsourced processes under 4.1.5, and validation of the software you use in the quality system itself under 4.1.6. It also carries the documentation architecture, which includes: the quality manual, the medical device file under 4.2.3, control of documents under 4.2.4, and control of records under 4.2.5.

Management Responsibility

Clause 5 runs from commitment, customer focus, and the quality policy in 5.1 through 5.3, into planning and quality objectives under 5.4, and then responsibility, authority, and the management representative under 5.5. It closes with management review under 5.6, which specifies both the inputs that have to go into the review and the outputs that have to come out of it.

Resource Management

Clause 6 covers human resources under 6.2, which is competence, training, and the effectiveness of that training, infrastructure and maintenance under 6.3, and the work environment and contamination control under 6.4.

Product Realization

Clause 7 is the largest of the 5 and the one auditors spend the most hours inside, covering planning and risk management under 7.1, design and development under 7.3, purchasing under 7.4, and production and service provision under 7.5, which brings in process validation under 7.5.6, sterile device requirements under 7.5.7, and traceability under 7.5.9. Control of measuring and monitoring equipment sits at 7.6.

Measurement and Improvement

Clause 8 closes the loop with feedback, complaints, and reporting to regulators under 8.2.1 through 8.2.3, internal audit under 8.2.4, process and product monitoring under 8.2.5 and 8.2.6, nonconforming product under 8.3, analysis of data under 8.4, and improvement, which is where CAPA sits, under 8.5.

Two threads run through all 5 of those clauses. The first is the risk-based approach to every process, which is stated at 4.1.2 and then assumed everywhere after it. The second is the phrase "applicable regulatory requirements", which appears dozens of times across the standard, and that phrase is precisely what allows a regulator to adopt this document as its own: the QMSR incorporates it, MDSAP audits against it, and the MDR grants a presumption of conformity through the harmonised EN version. The sub-clauses carried in bold across the next 2 slides are the ones auditors spend their time on, and those are the ones we are going to work through together.

The Audit-Critical Clauses

So now that we have established the shape of the standard, we can begin to look at the clauses where audits are actually decided. So now you may be asking, which of these clauses decides an audit? These 6 rows are the answer, and the middle column is the auditor's request list while the right-hand column is the pattern of failure. It is important to know that every item in that middle column is something you should be able to produce within minutes for the product the auditor has just picked up.

The first row I want to discuss today is: outsourced processes and suppliers, under 4.1.5 and 7.4. The auditor will ask for the approved supplier list, the evaluations, the quality agreements, and the incoming inspection records for the components in the unit they have sampled. The failures here are predictable, which are evaluations years out of date, agreements silent on change notification and on regulator access to the supplier's site, and scorecards nobody has ever verified.

The medical device file under 4.2.3, which much of the American side of the room knows as the Device Master Record, or DMR, is requested for the sampled device specifically, which means the current specifications, drawings, labeling, instructions for use, process specifications, and acceptance criteria. It fails when uncontrolled drawings are in use on the floor, and when the file does not match what is being built today.

Under 4.2.4 and 4.2.5, the auditor works through revision history, control of obsolete documents, completeness of the Device History Record, or DHR, and the retention and legibility of records. The failures here are small ones that carry real weight, which include handwritten "corrections" with no initials and no date, missing signatures, and unapproved forms still in circulation.

Management review is tested against the inputs required by 5.6.2, which include internal audits, complaints, regulatory reporting, and supplier performance, and then against the decisions, the resource actions, and the follow-up those inputs produced. It fails as a slide deck with no decisions in it, overdue actions, and a review in which regulatory reporting was never discussed.

For 6.2, the request is narrower than most manufacturers expect, because the auditor wants the training matrix and the effectiveness evaluation for the operators they have just finished interviewing. Two failures recur, which are operators trained on a superseded revision of a work instruction, and a "read and understood" signature with no effectiveness check behind it.

Design and development under 7.3 brings requests for design changes under 7.3.9, verification and validation, the risk management file, and the evidence of design transfer. The dominant failure here is a production or supplier change made with no design impact assessment and no risk impact assessment attached to it. Don't wait for an auditor to test that one. Pull a unit yourself and try to assemble the middle column against it.

SLIDE 11

Process validation under clause 7.5.6 is the row where the auditor asks for the installation qualification, the operational qualification, and the performance qualification, the IQ, OQ and PQ, for the process being observed that day, together with your revalidation triggers and the software validation for any automated equipment. The failure mode here is new equipment or new software running on the old validation package, which manufacturers discover at the moment the auditor asks which configuration was qualified, and the quieter failure is that no revalidation criteria were ever defined, so nobody can say what change would have obligated a requalification.

Traceability under clause 7.5.9 is the row that decides unannounced audits, and the reason is that the auditor has product in hand. They will ask for the full trace of the lot they sampled, which means the components and component lots that went in, the build records, the test results, the release decision, and the distribution records showing where each unit went. Where manufacturers fail is that the lot cannot be reconstructed within the audit day, or the distribution record is incomplete, and the reality is that if you cannot reconstruct the lot on the day, everything else is theory.

Clause 7.6, monitoring and measuring equipment, is more literal than manufacturers expect, because the question is the calibration status and records for every gauge and every tool the auditor sees in use. Overdue calibration is a finding by itself, while overdue calibration with no assessment of the product accepted on that gauge is a significantly worse one. I will always caution manufacturers about the uncalibrated tool on the bench marked "reference only" that inspectors have quietly started using for acceptance decisions.

The last 3 rows are complaints and reporting under 8.2.2 and 8.2.3, nonconforming product under 8.3, and corrective action under 8.5.2. For complaints, the auditor asks for the complaint log, the investigation records, the reportability decisions with their rationale, and the submission timelines. For nonconforming product, it is the nonconformance report log, the dispositions, the rework instructions required under 8.3.4 with their re-verification, and the concession records, and for corrective action it is your open and closed corrective and preventive actions, or CAPAs, with root cause analysis, action plans, and effectiveness verification.

It is important to note that across all 3 of those rows, the failure is rarely the absence of a system, but rather an undocumented decision. No rationale is recorded behind a decision of "not reportable", complaints are closed without an investigation, product is dispositioned "use as is" with no risk rationale, and rework goes back into stock without re-verification. Repeat nonconformances are never trended into a CAPA, the fix addresses the symptom instead of the cause, training becomes the universal corrective action, CAPAs sit open past a year with no approved justification, and there is no data behind the word "effective".

An unannounced auditor walks onto the floor and picks up a unit, which may be finished product, in-process product, or a lot sitting in stock. From that unit they pull the thread backwards.

SLIDE 15

The Mechanics

An unannounced audit by a notified body is typically 2 auditors for at least 1 audit day, and it takes place either at your manufacturing site or at a critical supplier or subcontractor named in your technical documentation. There is no agenda in advance and the auditors expect to be admitted immediately, which in practice means that whoever is sitting at reception at 08:10 on a Tuesday is part of your quality system.

Delay is written into the report, and I want to be precise about that, because a badly handled arrival is not a neutral event. The time between the knock at the door and the opening meeting becomes a documented fact in a document you do not control. Refusal of access is grounds for suspension of your certificate. Product may also be tested on site in front of the auditors, or samples may be taken away for testing elsewhere.

There is one obligation in the mechanics column that manufacturers forget until it costs them, which is that you must have told your notified body when your lines will not be running. That covers holiday shutdowns and campaign manufacturing where a given product only runs for a few weeks in the year. If they arrive and nothing is being made, that is a wasted audit day for them and a significant problem for you.

The Focus

What they want to see is whether the certified device is being made here, and made as it was certified, which means units pulled from the line or from stock and checked against the technical documentation, traceability of critical components and materials, the recent design, process, or supplier changes and whether the notified body was notified of them, the validation status of the processes that are actually running that day, and interviews with the production and inspection staff.

That last item deserves a sentence of its own, because manufacturers prepare documents and they don't prepare people. The auditors will have conversations with the people at the bench, and the answer an operator gives about what they do when a part looks wrong either matches your procedure or it does not.

The callout at the bottom of the slide states it more plainly than I can. They are not there to read your quality manual. They are there to see whether the product being built today matches the product you certified.


SLIDE 16

The First 60 Minutes

In order to keep an unannounced audit from becoming a finding before it has properly started, it is essential that the first 60 minutes on site are governed by a written protocol and not by reaction, and the version in front of you is reproduced in full in the Participant Handout, which means nobody needs to copy it down. We will walk the 4 phases from left to right.

Reception

The first 5 minutes belong to whoever is sitting at the front desk. Identity is verified before anything else, which means photographic identification, agency or notified body credentials, and for an FDA investigator, Form FDA 482. Reception then makes exactly 1 call, to the Audit Response Lead or to the named deputy on the 24/7 roster, and then escorts and waits.

Auditors are never left unattended, they are never refused, and they are never stalled while somebody upstairs works out what to do. A laminated script at the front desk is the cheapest readiness investment you will ever make, and it is the one thing most sites do not have.

Mobilize

Mobilization is the phase where readiness either exists already or does not exist at all. Auditors are escorted to the audit room and offered water and wifi as per your own policy, while the notifications go out in parallel to the site head, to quality assurance and regulatory affairs leadership, to corporate quality, and to legal.

At the same time the request log is opened, the scribe, the runner and the subject matter experts are assigned by name, and the back room is established with the appropriate personnel, system access, and printing capability already in place.

Opening Meeting

The opening meeting is where the boundaries of the day are set, and manufacturers who rush it or skip it spend the rest of the audit negotiating from behind. We confirm the scope, the standard and the regulation being audited against, the sites included, and the auditors' own plan for the day. You then give the site overview along with the environmental health and safety and gowning briefing, which you owe them as visitors on your floor.

The logistics agreed in that room include the rooms themselves, printing, system access, your photo and sample policy, and the route by which document requests and findings will be communicated to you during the day. Agreeing that communication route at the opening meeting is what lets you correct a misunderstanding at 11 in the morning instead of discovering it at the closing meeting.

On the Floor

Once they are on the floor, the auditors select the product and the audit follows it. Every sample taken and every document requested is logged with a timestamp, which gives you the reconciliation you are going to need later that week. Your subject matter experts answer what is asked and then stop, while the scribe records every statement made in the room.

Runners retrieve the Device History Record, the Device Master Record and the validation records, and factual errors are corrected immediately and politely, at the moment they are made and not at the end of the day.

I want to highlight 1 number before we go on, which is 50 minutes. That is how long the auditors in our first case study sat in a lobby, and we will come back to what those 50 minutes cost that company when we reach the case studies.

Audit Response Roles

So now that we have established how the first hour should run, we can move on to who is actually running it. There are 8 roles on this slide, and in a small company 1 person may reasonably hold 2 of them, but every role carries a name and every name carries a named deputy behind it. The roster covers nights, weekends, and holiday shutdowns, and reception holds it on paper, because an auditor arriving at 7 on a Monday morning does not wait while you look for a phone number in a system nobody has logged into yet.

The Audit Response Lead owns the audit and is the single voice to the auditors, which includes deciding what is provided and when. The site head carries resources and decisions and is visible at both the opening and the closing meeting. Front-room SMEs come from production, quality control, engineering, supplier quality, and regulatory affairs, and they answer what is asked and nothing more.

So now you may be asking, who decides whether a document is fit to go in front of an auditor? That is the back room. The back-room coordinator reviews every document before it goes into the front room, spots the issue inside it first, and prepares the SME before that person walks in, and the runners retrieve records on the strict understanding that nothing reaches the front room unreviewed. Document control and IT hold system access, controlled copies, record retrieval, and printing, and reception and security hold the script, the roster, the badges and the escort, which is to say they hold the first 5 minutes.

The scribe is the most under-rated role here. A verbatim log of the questions asked, the answers given, the documents shown, and the samples taken is what allows you to challenge a finding accurately at the closing meeting, and to write a precise response afterwards rather than a defensive one.

SLIDE 19

The 72-Hour Plan

The audit is not over when the car leaves the car park, and what your organization does in the following 72 hours largely determines the quality of the response that regulator or notified body eventually reads.

Day zero is the same day. Debrief within 2 hours, while memories are fresh and while the scribe's log still makes sense to the people who were in the room. Reconcile the request log against the auditors' list of findings, verbatim, and secure copies of everything you provided. Senior management is briefed that day, and you make any notification your procedures or your contracts require, which may include corporate, the Person Responsible for Regulatory Compliance (PRRC), and your other sites.

Day 1 is classify and contain. Classify each finding, name an owner and a date against every one of them, and contain anything that touches product or patient safety before you do anything else. For FDA, the clock that matters on a Form FDA 483 is 15 business days, which is the window for the agency to consider your response before it reaches a decision on a Warning Letter.

Days 2 and 3 are root cause and plan. Root cause analysis, not symptom fixes, and a clear distinction between a correction and a corrective action, with your effectiveness criteria defined up front rather than reverse-engineered later into whatever the data happened to show. The response plan goes to the notified body inside its stated window, and you should check your own contract for that number, while for the Medical Device Single Audit Program, or MDSAP, you should expect around 15 calendar days for the plan.

After that you implement, you collect objective evidence of closure, you verify effectiveness with data rather than with an assertion, and you feed the findings, the trends and the lessons into management review and into the next internal audit plan. It is important to remember that a finding closed without effectiveness data is a finding you will see again, and a repeat costs you considerably more than the original did.


SLIDE 20

Findings and Consequences by Regime

Before the case studies I want to give you the vocabulary of findings, because each regime expresses them differently and escalates them differently.

FDA works in inspectional observations recorded on a Form FDA 483, with the inspection then classified as No Action Indicated, Voluntary Action Indicated, or Official Action Indicated, and escalation running to a Warning Letter and then to seizure, injunction, consent decree, and import alert for foreign sites. What escalates an FDA inspection is a repeat observation, an inadequate 483 response, a data integrity problem, or a failure to report.

Notified bodies under the Medical Device Regulation work in minor and major nonconformities, with the definitions coming from the notified body itself and from MDCG guidance, and they work through corrective action plans and, at the far end, suspension or withdrawal of the certificate. What escalates with a notified body is a systemic major, refusal of access, an unreported significant change, or a nonconformity found on the sampled product.

MDSAP grades every nonconformity from 1 to 5 using the GHTF nonconformity grading method, and the arithmetic is worth hearing once. Direct-impact clauses start at grade 3 and indirect clauses start at grade 1. Add 1 grade where there is no documented process, and add 1 more for a repeat, capped at 5. A grade 4 or 5 is notified by the auditing organization to all 5 participating regulators within 5 business days, and those regulators may then act independently. Case 4 lands on a grade 4.

SLIDE 31

Global QMS Architecture: Core Plus Annex

Building a quality management system that holds up in every market at once is an exercise in deciding what has to be identical everywhere and what is allowed to be local, and the 4 tiers on this slide are how that decision gets written down. The organizing principle is that ISO 13485 is the spine, and that regulatory requirements are referenced rather than copied into your procedure text, which stops one market's change to one timeline from invalidating documents across the whole system.

The First Tier is the Global Quality Manual and Policies. This is one scope statement, one process map, and one site register, and I mean one of each, because the moment a site maintains its own quality manual you have created a second system that nobody reconciles.

The Next Tier is the Global Procedures, one procedure per process, each with a named global process owner behind it. The processes that belong at this tier include: complaints, corrective and preventive action, change control, supplier controls, design controls, document control, training, management review, and internal audit. A named owner is essential, because a procedure owned by a committee is a procedure that nobody keeps current, and divergence between sites is exactly what produced the systemic finding in Case 4.

The Third Tier is Where Locality Is Allowed to Live. Site work instructions describe how the work is physically done on that floor, with that equipment, and alongside them sits one Country Regulatory Annex per market which lists only the deltas for that market: the timelines, the forms, the authorities, the language requirements, and the roles.

Finally the Last Tier is Records and Forms, and the rule at this tier is one form per process globally. Local-language versions are controlled as translations and never as variants, because an uncontrolled local variant becomes a second process carrying the same document number.

A comprehensive governance structure is what stops the architecture quietly decaying between certification cycles, and the elements on the right include: global process owners, site quality heads accountable for Tier 3, a regulatory-intelligence feed wired into change control with an impact assessment completed within 30 days, a quarterly global quality council, and an annual review of the scope, the site register, and the requirements matrix. That 30-day impact assessment is the element that turns a regulatory change from an email somebody read into a controlled change.

Follow product exactly as a notified body would, which means reception, the opening meeting, a sample taken from the line, a trace through the Device History Record and the Device Master Record, a supplier file, and the complaint and corrective and preventive action history.

Grade honestly against notified body or MDSAP definitions, time every retrieval, and interview your operators. Debrief within a day, put the findings into CAPA and the readiness KPIs into management review, and repeat the exercise yearly at every site and at your top critical suppliers.

The first mock audit is always humbling, and that is the point, because a humbling Tuesday you chose costs far less than the Tuesday your notified body chooses.


SLIDE 37

I want to highlight the 5 sentences that I would like you to be repeating to each other in the car park.

Readiness Is a State, Not an Event

Manufacturers should assume that auditors arrive tomorrow at 08:00, and should build appropriate routines into the quality system instead of preparation projects tied to a date on a calendar.

Auditors Follow Product

The evidence chain that runs from a single unit on the line back to design and to your suppliers, and forward to the patient, is the chain you have to master, because it is the chain they will walk.

The First Hour Sets the Verdict

It is critical that a site has a documented reception script, a 24/7 roster, defined response roles, and named deputies, because the people sitting at reception are a working part of your Quality Management System.

Findings Are Root-Cause Problems

Fix the system, verify effectiveness with data, and write your records to be read.

A Global QMS Is One Process With Local Annexes

Harmonize the process, localize the annex, and govern the interfaces.

Those are the 5 sentences that survive contact with a real audit day.

Your 30-60-90 day plan begins with what is cheap and immediate.

In the first 30 days, write the reception script and the 24/7 roster with deputies, build the audit go-kit, inventory your quality agreements for their access and notification clauses, and tell your notified body your shutdown calendar.

The go-kit itself should include: your certificates, an organization chart, a site map, a procedure index cross-referenced to the ISO clauses and to each regulation that applies to you, a contact list, a blank request log, visitor and confidentiality forms, and your photograph and sample policy.

Days 31 through 60 are for the drills and the checks. Run the 30-minute Device History Record and traceability drill and fix whatever breaks, complete a QMSR and MDR overlay gap check against that procedure index, fix your calibration recall and your change-impact form, and build version 1 of the regulatory requirements matrix.

Days 61 through 90 are where you prove it. Run a mock unannounced audit at 1 site and at 1 critical supplier, add the readiness KPIs to management review, retire duplicate site procedures onto the global tier, and schedule the next drill along with the annual cycle.
